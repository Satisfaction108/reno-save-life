Kidney donation

Giving a kidney, and what it really involves

Most people are born with two kidneys but only need one. A living donor can give one to someone whose kidneys have failed. This page explains who it helps, how the process works and what's actually true about it. It's a personal decision, and nobody should feel pressured into it.

~6,500living people donated a kidney in 2025

Who a donated kidney helps

People on dialysis

When kidneys fail, dialysis filters the blood instead. It usually means three sessions a week, about four hours each, for as long as someone waits.

A kidney that lasts longer

A kidney from a living donor usually works for longer than one from a deceased donor, and the transplant can be planned instead of waiting for a call.

A shorter wait

Waiting for a deceased donor kidney can take several years. A living donor can cut that wait to months.

Even strangers

If you're not a match for someone you know, a paired exchange swaps donors between pairs so both people get a kidney. Some donors give to someone they've never met.

How living donation works

  1. 1

    Learn and talk it over

    Read about the risks and recovery, and talk with your family. Donors must usually be at least 18.

  2. 2

    Contact a transplant center

    Fill out a health questionnaire, either for a specific person or as a donor to anyone who needs a kidney.

  3. 3

    Get tested

    Blood and urine tests, blood pressure checks, a kidney scan and a check of your blood type and tissue match.

  4. 4

    Meet the team

    A doctor, a social worker and an independent donor advocate, whose only job is to look out for you, talk with you about your health and your decision.

  5. 5

    Decide

    You can say no at any point, even right before surgery. The center keeps your reason private.

  6. 6

    Surgery and recovery

    Most donor surgeries use small cuts. Donors usually leave the hospital in 1 to 3 days and return to normal activity in a few weeks.

  7. 7

    Follow-up

    The center checks your kidney function and blood pressure for about two years after donating.

The recipient's insurance covers the donor's testing and surgery. The National Living Donor Assistance Center can help with travel and lodging costs.

What transplant centers look for

Every center has its own rules, but most donors need to be in good overall health. These are the same things that protect your kidneys whether or not you ever donate.

Check my kidney health
  • Normal kidney tests (eGFR and urine protein)
  • Healthy blood pressure, or well controlled with little medicine
  • No diabetes
  • A healthy weight
  • Not smoking, or willing to quit
  • Good mental health and people to support your recovery

Myths and facts

MythYou need two kidneys to live a normal life.

One healthy kidney can do the work of two. After donation, the remaining kidney grows and takes on more of the load. Long-term studies show donors generally live as long as other healthy people.

MythDonors have to pay for the surgery.

The recipient's insurance pays for the donor's evaluation, surgery and follow-up care. Programs like the National Living Donor Assistance Center help with travel and other costs.

MythYou can only donate to a family member.

Friends, coworkers and complete strangers donate too. If you aren't a match for the person you want to help, a paired exchange can still get them a kidney.

MythRecovery takes months.

Most donors spend 1 to 3 days in the hospital. Many go back to school or desk work in 2 to 4 weeks, and to heavier activity in about 6 weeks.

MythWomen can't have children after donating.

Many women have healthy pregnancies after donating. There is a somewhat higher chance of high blood pressure during pregnancy, so doctors watch more closely.

MythIf a donor later needs a kidney, they go to the back of the line.

The opposite is true. People who have donated a kidney get priority on the national waiting list if they ever need one.

MythDonating has no risks at all.

Any surgery carries some risk, and donors have a small increase in their long-term chance of kidney problems. That's why the testing is so thorough and why centers explain your own risk before you decide.
